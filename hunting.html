Coast Range Hunting

Oregon's Last
Wild Timber

The Oregon Coast Range is not the Rockies. There are no open meadows, no glassing from ridgelines, no long-distance shots across alpine basins.

This is thick, wet, coastal timber — second-growth Douglas fir, old-growth Sitka spruce, and tangled alder bottoms wrapped in fog. Visibility is measured in yards, not miles. Sound carries through the canopy. Trails are elk trails, carved into hillsides that drop hundreds of feet to creek bottoms.

This is hunting that rewards patience, fitness, and local knowledge. Corey has been walking this timber since childhood — he knows the benches where elk bed, the saddles they cross, and the creek bottoms where blacktail feed at first light. That knowledge is the difference between a hard hunt and a productive one.

Into the Timber

Big Game

Roosevelt Elk

The Roosevelt elk is the largest subspecies of elk in North America, and Oregon's Coast Range holds one of the densest populations on the continent. These are not open-country animals — they live in the thick of coastal timber, bedding in old-growth stands and feeding in clear-cut regrowth and creek bottoms.

Coast Range elk hunting is close-range work. Shots are typically 50–150 yards through timber openings. You'll be reading sign, following trails through dense brush, and glassing small clearings where bulls stage during the rut. This is spot-and-stalk hunting at its most intense — slow, quiet, and demanding.

Corey hunts a mix of private timber company land and public BLM ground that he's scouted for decades. He knows the migration corridors, the bedding benches, and the feeding areas that produce year after year.

A Day in the Timber

Typical Elk Hunt Day

4:30a

Meet & Gear Check

Meet at the trailhead. Final gear check, review the day's plan, discuss wind direction and approach. Coffee in the dark.

5:15a

Hike In

Move into position before first light. Slow, quiet approach along elk trails and logging roads to glassing points or known staging areas.

6:00a

First Light

Glass clear-cuts, meadow edges, and creek bottoms. Listen for bugling, breaking branches, and cow calls. The first two hours of light are the highest-percentage window.

8:00a

Push Timber

If no elk at first light, transition to still-hunting through bedding timber. Slow movement through thick cover, reading fresh sign — tracks, rubs, wallows, and droppings.

12:00p

Midday Break

Lunch on the ridge. Reassess the morning, adjust strategy. Elk bed down midday in thick cover — this is a good time to rest, glass, and plan the afternoon approach.

3:00p

Afternoon Hunt

Reposition for the evening feed. Elk begin moving again 2–3 hours before dark, heading from bedding timber to feeding areas. This is often the best opportunity.

Dark

Debrief

Hike out. Review the day, discuss tomorrow's strategy. If an elk is down, field processing begins immediately.

Blacktail Country

Coastal Deer

Columbian Blacktail

The Columbian blacktail is the ghost of the Pacific Northwest timber. Smaller than a mule deer, more elusive than a whitetail, and evolved for dense coastal forest. They thrive in the tangled transition zones between old-growth and clear-cut — places where visibility drops to nothing and patience is the primary skill.

Blacktail hunting on the Coast Range is spot-and-stalk in its truest form. You're working edges — the border between timber and openings, old roads grown over with alder, creek bottoms where deer feed at dawn and dusk. Shots are close. A 100-yard shot is a long one.

Corey targets blacktail on a mix of private and public land that he's hunted since he was a kid. He knows the feeding patterns, the travel corridors, and the bedding cover that holds deer consistently across years and conditions.

Big Game

Black Bear

Oregon's Central Coast Range holds a healthy and growing black bear population. These bears live in some of the thickest timber on the continent — second-growth Douglas fir and alder thickets where visibility is measured in feet. They're adapted to the dense, wet landscape and are masters of disappearing into cover.

Fall bear hunting coincides with berry season and salmon runs. Bears feed heavily along creek corridors and in clear-cut regrowth where huckleberries and blackberries concentrate. This creates predictable patterns — glassing berry patches at dawn and dusk, and working salmon-bearing streams where bears come to feed.

Oregon prohibits the use of hounds and bait for bear hunting. All bear hunting is spot-and-stalk — which makes it honest, challenging, and deeply rewarding. Spring season targets bears emerging from dens on south-facing slopes; fall season is the higher-percentage window.

What to Expect

Bear Hunt Breakdown

Fall Season (Aug–Dec)

The primary bear hunting window. Bears are in hyperphagia — eating 20,000+ calories per day to prepare for denning. Berry patches in clear-cuts and salmon runs on coastal streams create concentrated feeding zones. Glassing at dawn and dusk, then moving in for the stalk.

Best months: September and October, when berries peak and salmon push upstream.

Spring Season (Apr–May)

Bears emerge from dens lean and hungry, gravitating to south-facing slopes where green-up starts first. Spring bears are more predictable in their travel patterns, working from den sites to early food sources. Shorter window, lower success rate, but a unique and rewarding hunt.

Physical demands: Moderate. Less vertical than elk, but expect 3–6 miles of hiking.

Regulations

Oregon black bear tags are available over the counter (OTC) for both spring and fall seasons. One bear per calendar year. No hounds, no bait, no trapping. Hunters must have a valid hunting license and bear tag.

Firearm: .270 Win or larger recommended. 6.5 Creedmoor and .30-06 are ideal choices for Coast Range distances.

Apex Predator

Apex Predator

Cougar

Oregon has one of the largest cougar populations in the western United States, with an estimated 6,000+ animals statewide. The Coast Range is prime cougar habitat — dense timber, healthy deer and elk herds as prey, and steep terrain that cougars use to their advantage.

Cougar hunting in Oregon is strictly spot-and-stalk. Hound hunting was banned by ballot measure in 1994. That makes cougar hunting one of the most challenging pursuits in North American big game. You're hunting an animal that is perfectly camouflaged, moves mostly at night, and has evolved to avoid detection.

Success requires patience, detailed knowledge of cougar travel routes, and the ability to identify fresh sign — scrapes, kills, and tracks. Corey knows the corridors where cats move through his hunting areas, and snow or fresh mud on logging roads can reveal activity overnight. This is hunting at its most demanding and rewarding.

Upland & Waterfowl

Wild Turkey

Rio Grande and Merriam's turkeys have established growing populations across Oregon's Central Coast. These birds thrive in the mixed habitat of the Coast Range — oak woodlands, open meadows, and timber edges that provide roosting, nesting, and feeding zones.

Spring gobbler season is the primary draw. There's nothing like hearing a tom thunder off the roost at first light, then working him in with calls until he's strutting at 20 yards. Oregon Coast timber makes this especially intense — limited visibility means you'll hear the bird before you see it, and the close encounters are electric.

Corey uses box calls, slate calls, and mouth diaphragms to work birds. He knows the roosting trees, strut zones, and travel routes that turkeys use consistently across seasons. Spring turkey hunting is accessible to all fitness levels and is an excellent introduction to hunting.

What to Expect

Turkey Hunt Breakdown

Spring Gobbler (Apr–May)

The marquee season. Toms are vocal and responsive to calling. Set up before dawn near known roost trees. When the first gobble echoes through the timber, the game is on. Calling sequences bring birds in close — most shots are under 40 yards with a shotgun.

Fall Season (November)

Less vocal, more challenging. Fall turkeys flock up and feed heavily on acorns and insects. Hunting involves scouting feeding areas, breaking flocks, and using kee-kee calls to reassemble scattered birds. A different game than spring, but equally rewarding.

Gear & Regulations

Shotgun with turkey choke (12 or 20 gauge) and #4–#6 shot. Full camo recommended. Oregon turkey tags are OTC with no draw. Spring limit: two bearded turkeys. Fall limit varies by unit. ODFW hunter education required.

Wing Shooting

Upland Birds

Oregon's Central Coast offers diverse upland bird hunting that most people don't know about. Mountain quail in the high Coast Range, California quail in lower elevation brush, ruffed grouse in the deep timber, and band-tailed pigeons — a unique coastal specialty that migrates through the region in fall.

Upland hunting here is walk-up work through mixed terrain — clear-cuts, timber edges, brushy draws, and old logging roads. A good bird dog transforms the experience. Ruffed grouse in old-growth timber are as challenging as any bird in North America — explosive flushes in tight cover where you've got a fraction of a second to mount and shoot.

Band-tailed pigeons are Oregon's only native migratory game bird, and the coastal corridor is one of the best flyways on the West Coast. Pass-shooting bandtails as they fly through river canyons is a unique experience you won't find inland.

Species Breakdown

What You'll Find

Mountain Quail

The largest quail species in North America. Found in brushy, steep terrain above 1,000 feet. These birds run more than they fly, making a dog essential. When they do flush, they go downhill fast. A true trophy upland bird.

Ruffed Grouse

The king of the timber. Ruffed grouse live in dense second-growth and alder bottoms. Flushes are explosive and close — 10 to 15 yards in thick cover. An open-choked 20-gauge is the weapon of choice. Expect a workout and a few missed shots.

Band-Tailed Pigeon

Oregon's only native migratory game bird. Band-tails fly through coastal river corridors in September and October, feeding on elderberries and cascara. Pass-shooting from ridge points is the method. Limit is two per day, with a special HIP registration required.

California Quail

Found in brushy lowlands, field edges, and blackberry thickets. Covey flushes of 15–30 birds are common. Fun, fast shooting with a 20-gauge or .410. Excellent for newer hunters and a great way to work young bird dogs.

Pacific Flyway

Waterfowl

Ducks & Geese

Oregon's Central Coast sits directly on the Pacific Flyway — one of North America's four major migratory waterfowl corridors. From October through January, millions of ducks and geese funnel through coastal estuaries, river bottoms, and flooded agricultural fields.

The Siletz Bay, Alsea estuary, and Yaquina Bay are prime hunting locations. Mallards, pintails, American wigeon, green-winged teal, and Canada geese are the primary species. Hunting methods include jump-shooting river sloughs, layout hunting in estuaries, and decoy spreads on flooded fields.

Waterfowl hunting is the most social hunt Rimewild offers. Groups of up to four can hunt together, and the action can be fast and sustained when birds are moving. Early morning hunts are the standard — you'll be set up before dawn and hunting through mid-morning.

Predator Hunting

Coyote & Bobcat

Predator hunting is the year-round option. When big game seasons close and the rivers are blown out, the Coast Range is still full of coyotes and bobcats that respond to electronic callers, hand calls, and decoys.

Coyotes are found throughout the Coast Range — in clear-cuts, meadow edges, and agricultural lowlands. They respond well to rabbit distress calls, pup distress, and coyote vocalizations. Electronic callers with remote speakers allow for hands-free calling and precise sound placement. Expect to set up at multiple stands per day, calling for 20–30 minutes at each before moving.

Bobcats are more elusive and require patience. They respond to rabbit and bird distress calls but come in slower and more cautiously than coyotes. Bobcat hunting is a long-game pursuit — fewer opportunities, but deeply satisfying when a cat commits to the call.

Oregon has no closed season and no bag limit on coyotes. Bobcat hunting requires a furbearer license. This is excellent off-season action and a great way to sharpen shooting skills.

Be Prepared

Physical
Requirements

Physical demands vary by species. Here's what to expect.

Elk (High): A typical elk hunt day involves 5–10 miles of hiking over steep, uneven terrain with elevation gains of 1,000–2,500 feet. Trails are often animal paths — narrow, slippery, and cut into hillsides. Expect to climb, descend, cross creeks, push through brush, and do it all in rain.

Blacktail & Bear (Moderate): Expect 3–6 miles of walking over mixed terrain, with stretches of standing, glassing, and slow-stalking through thick cover. Bear hunting involves longer sits at glassing points but can include steep approaches to feeding areas.

Cougar (High): Similar demands to elk hunting. Cougar terrain is steep, and covering ground to find fresh sign requires endurance and sure footing.

Turkey & Upland (Low–Moderate): Turkey hunting involves walking to setup points and then sitting. Upland bird hunting is walk-up work — 3–5 miles at a moderate pace over variable terrain. Accessible to most fitness levels.

Waterfowl & Predator (Low): Minimal walking. Waterfowl involves wading to set decoys and sitting in a blind. Predator hunting is drive-to, set-up, call, and repeat. Both are accessible to all fitness levels.

If big game goes down: Expect an additional 2–4 hours of hard physical work — field dressing, quartering, and packing meat out of steep timber. A bull elk can yield 250+ pounds of boneless meat. A bear or deer is lighter but may still be in challenging terrain.

Regulations & Licensing

Oregon
Requirements

All hunters must comply with ODFW regulations. Rimewild handles the logistics — you handle the paperwork.

Elk Tags — Controlled Hunt

Roosevelt elk in the Coast Range units require a controlled hunt tag, obtained through ODFW's annual draw. Applications open in early spring for the fall season.

Key dates:
Application deadline: typically mid-May
Draw results: typically late June
Season: November rifle
Units: varies by year — Corey advises on best units

Preference points accumulate if unsuccessful. Corey can advise on unit selection to maximize draw odds.

Deer Tags — General Season

Columbian blacktail deer tags for western Oregon are available over the counter (OTC). No draw required.

Requirements:
Oregon hunting license (resident or non-resident)
Western Oregon deer tag
Hunter education certificate

Non-resident licenses and tags can be purchased online through ODFW's licensing system.

Bear, Cougar & Turkey

All three species have OTC tags available — no draw required. Black bear: one per calendar year. Cougar: one per calendar year, year-round season. Turkey: spring and fall seasons with separate bag limits.

Key rules:
No hounds or bait for bear or cougar
Bear: must present skull for mandatory check
Cougar: must present skull and hide
Turkey: bearded birds only (spring)

Waterfowl & Upland

Waterfowl requires a federal duck stamp, state waterfowl validation, and HIP registration. Upland birds require only a standard hunting license. Band-tailed pigeons require a separate HIP registration.

Non-toxic shot: Required for all waterfowl. Steel shot is standard. Bismuth and tungsten are allowed alternatives.

Predator: Coyote — no closed season, no bag limit, hunting license only. Bobcat — furbearer license required, Dec–Feb season.

Non-Resident Hunters

Oregon welcomes non-resident hunters. License and tag costs are higher than resident rates.

Approximate costs (non-resident):
Hunting license: ~$168
Deer tag (OTC): ~$340
Elk tag (if drawn): ~$565
Bear tag: ~$271
Turkey tag: ~$35

Legal Requirements

All hunters must wear a minimum of one article of fluorescent hunter orange or fluorescent chartreuse during rifle seasons (not required for archery, turkey, waterfowl, or predator).

Required for all hunts:
Valid Oregon hunting license
Species-specific tag or stamp
Hunter education certificate
Photo ID in the field
Compliance with all ODFW regulations

Frequently Asked

Hunting
FAQ

What's the success rate for elk hunts?+
Roosevelt elk success rates on the Oregon Coast Range vary by unit and year, but generally range from 10–25% for rifle season. Corey's local knowledge and access to quality ground improves those odds significantly, but elk hunting is never a guarantee. It's a hunt, not a harvest.
What if I don't draw an elk tag?+
Your preference points accumulate. We can plan for the following year, or you can book a blacktail deer, bear, or turkey hunt instead — those tags are available over the counter with no draw required. Many hunters combine a deer or bear hunt with an elk application strategy.
How close are the shots?+
In Coast Range timber, most big game shots are 50–150 yards. Longer shots are rare — the terrain is too thick. Turkey and upland shots are under 40 yards with a shotgun. Practice shooting from field positions (kneeling, using a tree rest) at these distances.
Can I combine multiple species in one trip?+
Absolutely. Fall is prime combo season — blacktail deer, black bear, and upland birds overlap in October and November. You can hunt deer in the morning and grouse in the afternoon. Contact us to build a custom combo trip.
What happens if I harvest an animal?+
Field dressing and quartering happen in the field with Corey's assistance. Meat is packed out to the vehicle in game bags. Rimewild can recommend local meat processors in the Lincoln City/Newport area for butchering and packaging. Taxidermy referrals are also available.
Is archery hunting available?+
Currently, Rimewild focuses on rifle and shotgun hunts. Archery elk hunting in coastal timber is extremely challenging due to the dense cover and limited visibility. If you're an experienced archery hunter, contact us to discuss options.
What about the rain?+
It rains on the Oregon Coast. A lot. October and November average 8–12 inches of rain each. Quality rain gear is non-negotiable. The upside: rain moves animals, muffles sound, and keeps casual hunters off the mountain. Some of the best hunting days are the wettest.
Can I bring my own bird dog?+
Yes. Well-trained bird dogs are welcome and encouraged for upland hunts. Your dog should be steady to flush, responsive to commands, and comfortable working in thick, wet cover. Discuss breed and training level with Corey before the hunt.
Where should I stay?+
Lincoln City, Newport, or Toledo offer lodging close to hunting areas. Corey can recommend specific places that work well for early-morning departures. Budget motels, vacation rentals, and campgrounds are all options.
Cancellation policy?+
Full refund with 14+ days notice. 50% refund with 7–13 days notice. Hunts cancelled due to dangerous conditions (wildfire, extreme weather) are rescheduled or fully refunded. Unsuccessful hunts are not refundable — this is hunting, and effort is the guarantee, not outcome.
